How the benchmark was run

This page explains how every number on this site was produced, the choices that shaped the results and their tradeoffs, and what the study did not measure. It also gives the list prices used, defines every term, and links to the data behind every chart.

Who ran the study

Estuary and CorrDyn ran the study jointly.

Estuary is a data movement platform. It is the apparatus for data replication in this study and loaded the data into all seven warehouses: one capture fed seven materializations from the same stored collection, so every engine received the same data. Estuary was not one of the systems measured or compared.

CorrDyn is a data and AI consultancy. Its clients consistently ask which data warehouse to use and how to size it as workloads grow, and no published benchmark was specific enough to support its recommendations. CorrDyn designed the benchmark, wrote the code, ran the benchmark and wrote the report.

The three query suites

The study contains 244 queries from three public benchmark suites.

  • TPC-DS (99 queries) is the data industry's standard test of business reporting: a large table of sales joined to smaller tables of customers, products, stores and dates.
  • DSB (102 queries) runs the same reports against the same tables with skewed data, so a few products account for most of the sales. It also adds three queries of its own (100 to 102) with no TPC-DS twin, including a self-join of the sales table.
  • ClickBench (43 queries) filters and counts over one very wide table of web page views, with no joins.

TPC-DS and DSB ran at four data volumes, about 1 GB, 10 GB, 100 GB and 1 TB. ClickBench ran on its one table of about 100 GB.

How queries were run

  • Single user. Each query ran once to warm the engine and then three measured times, and the site reports the median of the three. Result caching was switched off wherever the engine allowed it.
  • Under load. For each level from 1 to 100 sessions, the benchmark opened that many independent connections and started all of their queries at the same instant. Workers drained a shared queue of queries, so the offered load fell as the queue emptied. Each scan level runs the suite's 43 queries once, so the 50-session level ran all 43 at once, and it is shown as 43. The 100-session level is left out: its extra connections had no queries to run, and on some engines they failed or timed out, which says nothing about how the engine handles query load. The join suites queue 201 queries (99 where only TPC-DS ran), so their levels start essentially full. Some sizes were not run up to 100 sessions, and for those the highest level run is a floor, not a measured limit.
  • Time limit. Every query had ten minutes. A query that did not finish in time, or that the engine or its connection ended with an error, is recorded as not completed.
  • Fixed compute. Each measurement used one compute size with automatic scaling switched off (Fabric's smoothing of bursts cannot be switched off), so each number describes what one unit of compute does with a given amount of data and a given number of people. ClickHouse ran three replicas under load, the vendor's default configuration; every other engine ran one unit.
  • Server-side time. Latency is the time the engine reports in its query history. Each vendor defines that time slightly differently, and the full results record the client wall-clock time beside it.
  • When. The runs took place between July and October 2026. The engines ran in different cloud regions.

How cost was obtained

Every engine is priced on one clock: a query costs the list rate of the compute it ran on, multiplied by the time the engine spent on it, its wait in the engine's own queue included and the network excluded. Under load, the queries that ran together split the time the compute was busy, and idle time between queries is charged to none of them. How directly the vendor's bill backs each figure is graded three ways, and the grade is displayed where cost figures are quoted:

  • Measured: the vendor's metered bill for the query itself (BigQuery on-demand).
  • Allocated: list rate times engine time, then capped or split against the capacity the vendor billed (Databricks, Redshift, Fabric, BigQuery reservations).
  • Modeled: list rate times engine time, with no invoice to check the total against (ClickHouse, MotherDuck, Snowflake).

Hourly prices are the vendors' published list rates, recorded during the runs and listed in full below. Negotiated and committed-use discounts are outside the study, so the costs are useful for comparing engines with each other and are not price quotes.

Six choices that influenced the results, and their tradeoffs

  1. Using one ingestion path for every engine removed per-engine loaders as a variable, at the cost of any engine's native bulk-load optimizations.
  2. Applying no physical tuning to any engine kept the comparison bounded, at the cost of favoring engines with strong defaults (ClickHouse's sorted index, Databricks' Predictive Optimization) and penalizing engines built to be tuned (Redshift, BigQuery).
  3. Holding compute fixed made every performance ceiling visible, at the cost of leaving unmeasured how well each engine scales out.
  4. Measuring server-side time removed network delay from the comparison, at the cost of each vendor defining that time slightly differently.
  5. Reporting the median of three warm runs hides less variance than reporting the fastest run, at the cost of measuring only warm engines, with their data caches full.
  6. Reporting cost per query with its method stated is more transparent than a single ranking, at the cost of making some cross-engine cost comparisons indicative only.

The limits of the study

  • Default physical layout everywhere. No clustering, distribution or sort keys, partitioning or hand-built statistics were applied on any engine, so a well-tuned deployment can improve on these results.
  • Translated SQL, with small correctness fixes. The join queries were translated mechanically from DuckDB's dialect for every engine, and five engines carry small hand-edits, such as rounding or an explicit cast, where a query otherwise returned wrong results. These are corrections, not tuning; no engine's join queries were rewritten for speed.
  • MotherDuck ran untranslated. The queries were written in DuckDB SQL and checked against DuckDB, so MotherDuck alone ran them without translation. The study cannot measure the size of that advantage.
  • Not measured: cold starts, data loading, writes, autoscaling, and data volumes above 1 TB. A warehouse is sized for the reporting tables people query, which are rarely larger than that; the cost of storing raw data and of the jobs that build the reporting tables needs a separate evaluation.
  • Three measured runs. That is enough for a reliable median and not enough to attach a statistical margin to each figure, so small differences between engines should be read as ties.
  • Not an audited TPC result. The TPC-DS and DSB workloads used here are derived from TPC-DS, and the results are not comparable to published TPC-DS results.
  • One instance for MotherDuck. Every session ran under one account and shared one Duckling. In production each user gets a separate instance.

Checking that every engine answered the same questions

The queries were validated in DuckDB first, and DuckDB's answers are the reference. At 1 GB, 197 of the 201 join queries were compared with DuckDB's results on every engine, within a small numeric tolerance; the other four were left out because engines may legitimately order tied rows differently. At the larger volumes, the check is that every engine returned the same number of rows for each join query. The scan queries use the SQL published in the ClickBench project for each engine, so, as ClickBench itself does, they were timed but not checked against DuckDB.

List prices as captured

Every hourly price on this site is the vendor's public list rate for the compute size named, as recorded in the benchmark's rate card during the July to October 2026 runs. MotherDuck lowered its published rate for the Giga size from $36.00 to $24.00 an hour after the runs, and every MotherDuck Giga price and cost here uses the new rate. Snowflake prices are Standard edition, $2 a credit, for Gen2 Standard warehouses. ClickHouse Cloud provisions one vCPU per 4 GiB of replica memory, so the replica label fixes the price at the Scale tier's $0.2985 an hour per compute unit of 8 GiB and 2 vCPU: a 3x64GB service is 24 units, $7.16 an hour. ClickHouse cost per query is engine time multiplied by that rate for the size that ran.

Challenge a result

If you reproduce a result, find a problem with the method, or test a configuration the study did not cover, please open an issue on GitHub with the setup and measurements, so others can evaluate them. A link from any chart's "Copy link" button opens this site with the same selection, which is the clearest way to point at a specific number.
