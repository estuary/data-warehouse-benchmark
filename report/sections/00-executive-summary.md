Estuary and CorrDyn jointly ran {{fact:engines_with_data}} cloud warehouses through {{fact:distinct_queries}} queries at {{fact:scale_count}} data scales, at five or more compute sizes per engine, from at or near each vendor's smallest size up to the sizes a 1 TB workload calls for, alone and under bursts of up to 100 sessions. Estuary's single ingest path is the control that makes the comparison fair: every engine received the same bytes through the same loader (Part I). The queries come in three suites, and each asks a different question: TPC-DS (99 queries) is star-schema analytics, a fact table joined to its dimensions over uniform data; DSB (102) keeps that schema, skews the distributions, and adds the corpus's only fact-to-fact joins; ClickBench (43) is scan-and-aggregate analytics over one wide table. The report's finding is not an ordering. It is that the {{fact:engines_with_data}} engines occupy different envelopes, and the configuration that is fastest for one analyst is rarely the one that serves fifty.

Call the unit of comparison an envelope: the region of work a single engine at a single compute size will admit and serve at a stated capacity. Every result in this report is one envelope. Horizontal scaling was pinned off by protocol on every engine (Part II), so each envelope describes how one fixed allocation admits and protects work at a size the operator sets. A scaled-out deployment would answer a different question, and none was run here.

Nothing here scales the way a buyer expects, and the intuition about size inverts with volume. Growing the data 10× costs {{fact:scaling_step_all_engines_range}} the time across the engines measured, never 10×. Growing the hardware pays back differently at each scale: a 16× span of Snowflake's ladder returns about 2× at 100 GB and {{fact:sf1000_full_ladder_speedup}} at 1 TB, while at 1 GB that same span is a slowdown. Part II states the three regularities behind this; their consequence is that price-performance inverts with scale, and the tier that is a bargain at 1 TB is waste at 1 GB even under concurrent load: at that scale, the concurrency grid shows a cheaper tier serving just as many sessions on every engine whose latency under load we publish.

Over-provisioning is measurable, and it is common. At 1 GB, Redshift's largest band runs {{fact:redshift_overprovision_pct}} slower than its 16 RPU band and costs {{fact:redshift_overprovision_price}} as much. On Redshift and Snowflake, the biggest tier at small data is the slow and expensive choice, so a buyer who reads the sizing dropdown as a speed control will pay more to wait longer. Sizing is a workload question, answered at the scale you run, and the number to size against is useful concurrency rather than solo latency.

Concurrency separates these engines far more than solo latency does. Across each engine's whole concurrency grid, the share of queries left unfinished runs from {{fact:conc_shed_redshift}} on Redshift to {{fact:conc_shed_worst}} on ClickHouse, and where an engine sheds, adding compute lowers the share: Databricks at 1 TB goes from most of the burst unfinished on its smallest warehouse to zero by the Medium tier. On the engines whose unfinished queries are all our ten-minute timeout firing rather than a warehouse error, a timeout tells you the query did not finish in ten minutes, not whether it waited in the warehouse's queue or never reached it. Latency under load is the engine's time on a query, its queue wait included and the network excluded. Databricks, Redshift and BigQuery report execution time only, so on all three the queue wait is added back from their job history. Part III's concurrency cut shows how each engine's bursts end, and Part II gives the useful-concurrency number to plan against.

Regime fit dominates the ranking. Change the workload's shape, from star-schema joins to wide-table scans, and the field reorders more than any compute-tier change moves it. Part III's regime map locates each engine, and its recommendation says how to choose before you compare a single latency number.

Reputations fail against measurement, and they fail in both directions. The two engines the conversation writes off hardest beat their reputations by the widest margins. Redshift, reputed to be the legacy engine you migrate away from, came second on decision support by geometric mean at every scale as an untuned baseline. MotherDuck, reputed to stop working past laptop scale, was the fastest decision-support engine by the same measure at every scale through 1 TB and finished every query it ran single-user, though its queueing curves are the steepest measured once sessions pile up on one shared instance. Part III sets all {{fact:engines_with_data}} reputations beside the data. The argument there is ours, and it is editorial by construction; every number beside it is measured, and appears in the report with its method attached.

The measurement discipline is itself a deliverable, and the last four findings are only trustworthy because of it. A query that does not finish is published as a result, so a reader can see where an engine gave out instead of inferring it from a gap. The completion matrix is a headline artifact. Every cost is built the same way, each engine's compute at its public list rate for the time the engine spent running the query (on Fabric, the capacity units it consumed when a query bursts past its SKU), and a confidence tier on each figure records how directly the vendor's billing confirms it, from the metered bill down to a rate applied to engine time. The build recomputes {{fact:verified_tables}} published tables from the results database every time, and it fails if one of them drifts (Part V). Where the method bends a result, the report says so where the result appears: the single loader, MotherDuck's home-field asymmetry, and a no-tuning policy applied uniformly to engines it does not affect uniformly (Part I).
